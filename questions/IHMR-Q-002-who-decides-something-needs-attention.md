# Who is allowed to decide that something needs attention?


If the system can say "this person needs a blood pressure check," something or someone made
that call. Who, and on what authority?

## Why it matters

This is where clinical accountability either holds or quietly dissolves. Get it wrong in one
direction and you have an automated system making clinical judgements nobody signed off. Get it
wrong in the other and you have a coordination layer so cautious it cannot coordinate anything.

## The questions inside the question

- Can a clinical guideline generate something automatically, without a clinician in the loop?
- Can an AI system propose one? Under what constraints, and visible to whom?
- When must a clinician approve before anything reaches the person?
- How are conflicting guidelines resolved, given they frequently disagree?
- When does something stop being relevant, and who decides that?
- Who is accountable when something important goes unresolved for a long time?

## What would count as an answer

A clear map of what may be generated by rule, what requires clinical review, what an AI may
suggest but never assert, and where accountability sits in each case. Ideally something a
medical council would recognise as reasonable.

## Related worry

We use the word "obligation" internally for the thing being created, and we are aware the word
is doing heavy lifting. It is meant as an operational primitive, not a legal duty and not
something anyone is forced to do. But words drift, especially once they become fields in a
database. See [IHMR-Q-003](IHMR-Q-003-what-can-a-person-decline.md).

## Help us with this

Clinicians and people who have worked on clinical governance or medical regulation: this is
yours. We would particularly like to hear where analogous systems have got this wrong.
