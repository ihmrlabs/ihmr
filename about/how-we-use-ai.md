# How We Use AI

**We use AI in this project, and we think telling you exactly how is part of using it well.**

Research, engineering, analysis, drafting - AI is involved in all of it. Our own central idea is
that AI changes what coordination costs, so refusing to use it in our own work would be a
strange position to hold.

The rule is not *whether*. It is **accountability and honesty**.

## A named human signs off on everything

Nothing here is published without a person who has read it, checked its claims against their
sources, and put their name to it.

Signing off means something specific:

> I have read this. I have checked its claims against their sources. If it is wrong, that is my
> error, not the tool's.

An AI cannot be accountable for a claim. It cannot be questioned by a journalist, corrected by a
professional body, or stand behind a number in front of a policymaker. In a project that is
asking you to trust claims about health, accountability has to land on a person.

## We always tell you how AI was used

Every document says so, at the top, where you cannot miss it.

**This is not a confession.** We think a project that uses AI well and says so plainly is more
trustworthy than one that uses it quietly. The only version of this we would treat as serious
misconduct is using AI and *not* saying so - not because using it is wrong, but because hiding
how work was made is.

| Label | What it means |
|---|---|
| `none` | No AI involved in making this document |
| `ai-assisted-editing` | AI helped with structure, clarity, or formatting. The substance is human |
| `ai-assisted-research` | AI helped find or summarise sources. Every claim was checked by the signer against the original |
| `substantially-ai-assisted` | AI wrote significant parts of the text. A human reviewed, verified, and signed off |
| `ai-generated-draft` | AI wrote the first draft. A human reviewed, verified, and signed off |

Where it is a close call, we declare the higher one. Over-disclosing costs us nothing.

These labels describe how the *text* was made. They say nothing about quality - a carefully
verified `ai-generated-draft` can easily be better than a carelessly written `none`.

## What AI does here

Finding and summarising sources · drafting and editing · parsing documents and datasets into
usable form · writing and reviewing code · building simulations and analysing results ·
arguing with our conclusions and finding the weak spots · maintaining our internal knowledge
base.

## What AI never does here

- **Count as evidence.** AI output is not a source. It helps us find sources; it never replaces
  reading them.
- **Verify anything.** Only the person signing off verifies. We will never tell you AI checked
  something.
- **Sign off.** See above.
- **Get listed as an author.** A tool we used is not a person to credit.
- **Produce clinical guidance.** We do not publish clinical guidance at all.
- **Generate volume for its own sake.** A large pile of unverified machine-written material
  would be a liability, not an achievement.

## The thing we are actually guarding against

AI can produce a document that is fluent, well-structured, properly formatted, plausibly cited,
and wrong. Generating one of those is much easier than spotting one.

Our defence is not avoiding AI. It is that **a named person has checked every claim against its
source, and their name is on it.** Where that has not happened, we do not publish. There is no
middle state.

We are setting this standard now, while the project is small, because it is not something you
can add later.

## If you spot something

If you find a claim here that does not hold up, please tell us - that is exactly the failure
this policy exists to catch, and we would much rather hear it from you than not hear it at all.
