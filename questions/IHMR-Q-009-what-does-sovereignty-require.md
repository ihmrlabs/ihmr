# What does sovereignty actually require here?


If something like this ever became real infrastructure, it would be the kind of system a
country cannot afford to have switched off by someone else.

## Why it matters

The principle we are working from:

> No single foreign corporation, foreign government, or infrastructure provider should be able
> to deny India access to, observe, or materially control a national health maintenance system.

That is a strong claim. What it means in practice is much less obvious.

## Four layers we distinguish

- **Data** - sensitive health information under Indian legal and technical control
- **Compute** - critical processing not solely dependent on infrastructure that can be
  externally denied
- **Models** - not unusable because one provider changes policy, pricing or access
- **Operations** - able to keep running through outages, disputes, sanctions or conflict

## The tension we have not resolved

The cost argument for this whole project assumes capable AI at low marginal cost. Sovereignty
pushes towards domestic, open weight, and on-device models. **Those are not currently the same
capability tier, and the gap costs money.**

We have been treating these as separate tracks. They are coupled, and the economics should
probably be worked out against the model constraints rather than against the best model
available anywhere.

## What would count as an answer

- Which specific workloads genuinely need sovereign inference, and which do not
- What forms of private and foreign participation are acceptable
- What actually happens if a major provider becomes unavailable
- How model portability is guaranteed rather than hoped for

Sovereignty does not require doing everything domestically. It requires not being unable to.

## Help us with this

People who have worked on critical national infrastructure, and anyone who has thought
carefully about running serious workloads on Indian compute.
