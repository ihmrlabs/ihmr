# What can a person decline, and what happens then?


A system built to make sure nobody is forgotten could very easily become a system that will not
leave anyone alone. We take this seriously.

## Why it matters

There is a distinction we think is important and have not yet made precise:

> The system may have a responsibility not to forget a person, while the person keeps every
> right to decline or disengage from any particular intervention.

Holding both halves of that at once is the hard part.

## The questions inside the question

- What exactly can someone decline? Everything? A specific screening? All contact?
- How should a decline affect what happens next, if at all?
- Is there any circumstance where something should be compulsory? Communicable disease is a
  genuinely different case from chronic disease, and we should say why.
- How is a decline represented so that it does not read as failure, non-compliance, or a
  problem to be solved?
- If a person declines something repeatedly, at what point does continuing to ask become
  harassment?

## Where our thinking currently sits

Some things we are fairly confident about. Declining must be a first-class outcome, not an error
state. Disagreement is not non-compliance. The system should know when to leave a healthy person
alone. Not every recommendation deserves to become a high priority.

## The structural risk we are watching

Once something is a record with a status and an accountable party attached, it starts behaving
like a compliance artefact whatever we intended. The gap between "the system thinks this needs
attention" and "this person is non-compliant" is uncomfortably small.

We think declining needs to be a proper terminal state with the same standing as completion,
rather than an exception path. We have not designed that yet.

## Help us with this

Privacy researchers, medical ethicists, and anyone who has watched a well-meaning system become
coercive in practice: please tell us what we are not seeing.
